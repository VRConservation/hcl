# Background
Hat Creek Lumber has been developing a community-scale sawmill in NE California since 2021. The sawmill will be integrated with Hat Creek Bioenergy LLC, a 3-megawatt bioenergy facility at the former Louisiana Pacific sawmill location near Burney, CA as shown in the original site plans:

:::{figure} images/site.png
:alt: hat creek site plans
:height: 700px
:::

The sawmill has been designed to manufacture lumber from small-diameter, lower-value logs. These logs are typically sold through federal timber sales, integrated resource service contracts, and Master Stewardship Agreements. 

Located NE of Burney, California, the business owners and Partners of Hat Creek Lumber want to structure the company as a unique public-private partnership (P3) with nonprofit entities (e.g., Fall River and Pit Resource Conservation Districts) to leverage public resources for the community and landscape. This P3 structure is intended to maximize the value of a public resource (i.e., sawlogs) and then direct profits from lumber sales to the RCDs or nonprofits. 

Private industrial timberland owners/managers sell logs, and Fire Safe Councils sell logs through fuel reduction activities. Existing infrastructure that processes fiber is primarily scaled for activities on private lands, not federal lands. In Shasta County, where the mill is located, federal land ownership is nearly 60%. Therefore, the greatest opportunity to increase log and biomass supply comes from federally managed lands. 

# Challenges
Building and sustainably running a community-based sawmill faces many challenges, including financial, institutional, forest resilience, and climatic ones.

## Finance
Securing equity and debt financing is the biggest hurdle to developing and establishing a sawmill or biomass facility. Financial entities want to know if a long-term feedstock supply contract exists. Stewardship contracts that provide this security are difficult to develop, so Hat Creek Lumber advocates partnering with existing implementing entities, such as the RCDs and other NGOs, that have master stewardship agreements with their respective Forests. However, for this approach to work, implementing entities must be responsible for managing thousands of acres, some of which include sawlog and biomass components. 

## Institutional
The region is dominated by public lands, so success depends on working across multiple agencies and organizations. Although the pace and scale of restoration are increasing, institutional inertia can be challenging, particularly for new ideas. We've mitigated this risk for decades through the Burney Hat Creek Collaborative, and all involved organizations support the mill.

## Forest Health
Over a century of fire suppression has created unhealthy forests prone to fire, disease, and other disturbances. Many small-diameter trees compete for scarce resources, creating a chronic state of stand 'malnutrition' and making forests vulnerable to disturbances such as fire or disease.

:::{figure} images/unthinned.png
:alt: unthinned forest
:::

When forests are thinned and fire is reintroduced, stands become more diverse, heterogeneous, and able to withstand unwanted disturbances.

:::{figure} images/thinned.png
:alt: thinned forest
:::

Treatment costs are typically high at first pass, given the long history of fire suppression, but costs decrease over time. However, returning for stewardship treatments, the easiest being managed wildfires or prescribed fire, is critical to maintaining healthy forest conditions.

# Solutions
The challenge is immense in scale. The NE California region where the project is located covers about 7.6 million acres (3.1 mn hectares) and has seen some of the West's largest wildfires in the past 10 years.

Fortunately, partners are implementing many forest health projects (yellow) across the Burney Hat Creek landscape (white). Third-party teams are working with forests (National Forest shown in green) with planning funds provided by Sierra Nevada Conservancy.

:::{figure} images/projects.png
:alt: treated forest
:::

Once implementation begins, many trees must be processed to break the cycle of severe wildfire. Organizations hope to sign a long-term feedstock agreement with Hat Creek Lumber.

The new ownership structure between Hat Creek Lumber and the implementing entity will legally allow for the purchase of the sawlogs and the gifting of revenue. The implementing entity will utilize its procurement policy and competitively bid the work (e.g., cut, skid, deck, and deliver) to the sawmill.

# Finance
Hat Creek Lumber's financial structure comes from local business investors, grants, and equity and debt financing. The Partner(s) will then use these funds, along with donations from philanthropic entities, voluntary carbon market sales, and grants, to reinvest in project work in the communities where the wood is sourced. This process maximizes community benefits and helps secure continued local support. Overall, the RCDs and NGOs project will focus on USFS lands to treat heavy fuels (e.g., mastication, Rx) and improve infrastructure (e.g., campgrounds, roads). Revenue will also support projects on private lands, particularly for small non-industrial timberland owners, to enhance community wildfire safety and promote community resilience. We can provide more details on the financing structure upon request.
