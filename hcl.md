# Background
Hat Creek Lumber has been developing a community-scale sawmill in NE California since 2021. The sawmill will be integrated with Hat Creek Bioenergy LLC, a 3-megawatt bioenergy facility at the former Louisiana Pacific sawmill location near Burney, CA ({numref}`site-plans`).

:::{figure} images/site.png
:label: site-plans
:height: 400px
This is the caption for a figure
:::

The sawmill has been designed to manufacture lumber from small-diameter, lower-value logs. These logs are typically sold through federal timber sales, integrated resource service contracts, and Master Stewardship Agreements. 

Located NE of Burney, California, the business owners and Partners of Hat Creek Lumber have the desire to structure the company in a unique public-private partnership (P3) with nonprofit entities (e.g., Fall River and Pit Resource Conservation Districts) to leverage the value of public resources for the community and landscape. This P3 structure is intended to maximize a public resource (i.e., sawlogs) and then provide the profits from the lumber sales to the RCDs or nonprofits. 

Logs are sold by private industrial timberland owners/managers and through fuel reduction activities by Fire Safe Councils. Existing infrastructure that processes fiber is primarily scaled for activities on private lands, not federal lands. In Shasta County, where the mill is located, land ownership is nearly 60% federal ownership. Therefore, the greatest opportunity for increases in log and biomass supply is from federally managed lands. 

# Challenges
Many challenges are faced in building and sustainably running a community-based sawmill, including financial, institutional, forest resilience, and climatic.

## Finance
Securing equity and debt financing is the greatest hurdle to overcome in developing and establishing a sawmill or biomass facility. Financial entities want to know if a long-term feedstock supply contract exists. Stewardship contracts providing this security are difficult to develop, so Hat Creek Lumber advocates a partnership with existing implementing entities such as the RCDs and other NGOs with master stewardship agreements with their respective Forests. However, for this approach to work, implementing entities need to be responsible for treating thousands of acres, some of which have a sawlog and biomass component. 

## Institutional
The region is dominated by public lands, making working across multiple agencies and organizations paramount to success. Although the pace and scale of restoration are increasing, institutional inertia can be challenging, particularly for new ideas. We've mitigated this risk for decades through the Burney Hat Creek Collaborative, and all involved organizations support the mill.

## Forest Health
Over a century of fire suppression has created unhealthy forests prone to fire, disease, and other disturbances.

Many small-diameter trees compete for scarce resources, creating a chronic state of stand 'malnutrition' and making forests vulnerable to disturbances such as fire or disease.

:::{figure} images/unthinned.png
:alt: unthinned forest
:::

When forests are thinned and fire is reintroduced into the system, forest stands are more diverse, heterogeneous, and able to withstand unwanted disturbances.

:::{figure} images/thinned.png
:alt: thinned forest
:::

Treatment costs are typically high at first pass, given the long history of fire suppression, but costs decrease over time. However, returning for stewardship treatments, the easiest being managed wildfires or prescribed fire, is critical to maintaining healthy forest conditions.

Once implementation begins, many trees must be processed to break the cycle of severe wildfire. Organizations hope to sign a long-term feedstock agreement with Hat Creek Lumber.

The new ownership structure between Hat Creek Lumber and the implementing entity will legally allow for the purchase of the sawlogs and the gifting of revenue. The implementing entity will utilize its procurement policy and competitively bid the work (e.g., cut, skid, deck, and deliver) to the sawmill.

# Finance
The financial structure of Hat Creek Lumber is the result of local business investors, grants, and equity and debt financing. The Partner(s) will then utilize these funds and donations from philanthropy entities, voluntary carbon market sales, and grants to reinvest into project work in the communities where the wood is sourced. This process maximizes community benefits, thereby gaining continued local support. Overall, the RCDs and NGOs project will focus on USFS lands to treat heavy fuels (e.g., mastication, Rx) and improve infrastructure (e.g., campgrounds, roads). Revenue will also be used on private lands, particularly small non-industrial timberland owners, to enhance community safety from wildfire and promote community resilience. More details on the finance structure can be provided upon request.
